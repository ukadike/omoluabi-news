Leopardus tilcayo: The Wild Cat Hidden in Bolivia's Cloud Forest

2026-09-17 · Omoluabi News — Research POC

A newly described tiger cat from Bolivia's Yungas shows how genomics, museum collections, field observation and local knowledge can redraw the map of biodiversity.

A cat that science had not named

On September 17, 2026, researchers formally described Leopardus tilcayo, a small spotted wild cat from Bolivia's Yungas cloud forests. The study in Current Biology used phylogenomics and museum material to revisit the South American tiger-cat complex and identify several distinct evolutionary lineages, including the Bolivian species.

The animal itself was not unknown to people living around it. Local communities already used the name tilcayo. The scientific change was therefore not the first human encounter with the cat; it was the accumulation of evidence needed to establish a separate evolutionary lineage.

The discovery chain

National Geographic reports that in 2016 Bolivian biologist Paola Nogales-Ascarrunz was contacted about an unusual cat at a wildlife sanctuary. A local man had found it as a kitten near forest and initially thought it was domestic. At Senda Verde Animal Refuge, the animal's markings raised questions about whether it fit existing descriptions of South American tiger cats.

Appearance alone could not settle the issue. Tiger cats are a useful example of a cryptic-species problem: closely related animals can look similar while carrying deep genetic differences. The research team compared genomic material across the tiger-cat complex, including living and museum specimens. Reporting on the study describes a dataset of 38 Leopardus specimens.

The phylogenomic analysis supported five distinct tiger-cat species and a newly described Peruvian subspecies, Leopardus tigrinus antisuyo. The lineage leading to L. tilcayo is estimated to have diverged from close relatives roughly 1.4 million years ago.

What is known — and what remains unknown

The best-documented individual highlighted by National Geographic is about 18 inches, or roughly 46 centimetres, from head to end of body and weighs about 3 pounds, or roughly 1.4 kilograms. Published images show a light-brown coat with dark irregular rosettes and a relatively slender build.

The knowledge base is still very small. Reuters reports only two examined living individuals plus a limited number of camera-trap records. Preliminary evidence suggests nocturnal activity, while rodent remains in scat indicate small rodents may be part of the diet. Researchers do not yet have a reliable population estimate, complete range map or detailed account of reproduction, prey, predators and habitat requirements.

Why the “first in a century” claim needs context

Several outlets describe L. tilcayo as the first newly identified living cat species in more than a century. The taxonomic distinction matters because felid classifications have changed repeatedly as known subspecies were split, merged or elevated. Here the researchers describe a lineage that had not previously been formally proposed as its own species or subspecies.

The conservation problem begins immediately

The Yungas are humid mountain forests on the eastern slopes of the Andes. Current reporting identifies deforestation, agricultural expansion and mining among pressures on the ecosystem. If L. tilcayo occupies a narrow range, assessing it separately from a broad tiger-cat category could materially change how its conservation risk is understood.

The research team has shared the revised taxonomy with the International Union for Conservation of Nature. Future assessments may therefore evaluate these lineages separately rather than under older, broader categories.

The Omoluabi test: who gets to say a species exists?

The evidence chain is more revealing than the headline “scientists discover a cat.” Local communities already had a name for the animal. A sanctuary held a living individual. A field biologist noticed a mismatch. Museum collections preserved biological evidence. Genomics connected those records across geography and supported a taxonomic revision.

Evidence chain: local knowledge → field observation → sanctuary record → morphological contradiction → museum specimens → genome comparison → taxonomic revision → conservation reassessment.

No single layer tells the entire story. This proof of concept preserves who knew what, when they knew it, which evidence changed the classification and what remains unresolved.

What Omoluabi should watch next

  1. Range: Where exactly does L. tilcayo occur within the Bolivian Yungas?
  2. Population: How many individuals remain, and are populations connected?
  3. Ecology: What does the cat eat, when does it hunt, and which habitats does it require?
  4. Genetic diversity: How much variation exists within the newly recognized species?
  5. Threat overlap: Which forms of land-use change intersect confirmed habitat?
  6. Conservation status: How will the IUCN assess the lineage independently?
  7. Local testimony: What additional names, observations and records exist in communities that have known the animal for generations?

The description of Leopardus tilcayo is less an endpoint than the opening of a dataset. The next task is to build enough knowledge to understand the conditions under which the species can persist.
