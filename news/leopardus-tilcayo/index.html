Who Gets to Name a Species? The Case of Leopardus tilcayo

2026-09-17 · Omoluabi News — Research POC

People in Bolivia's Yungas already had a name for the tilcayo. In 2026, genomics gave that animal a new status inside formal science. Omoluabi follows the difference between being known, being documented and being officially classified.

The headline says “discovered.” The evidence says something more complicated.

On September 17, 2026, researchers formally described Leopardus tilcayo, a tiny spotted wild cat from Bolivia's Yungas cloud forests. Genomic analysis placed the Bolivian population on a distinct evolutionary branch within the South American tiger-cat complex, separated from close relatives by roughly 1.4 million years.

But “discovered” is not the same thing as “first known.” National Geographic reports that local people already called the animal tilcayo. When Bolivian biologist Paola Nogales-Ascarrunz asked why, people told her the name had been passed down through generations. The scientific paper did not create the animal, the encounter or the local name. It changed the animal's status inside a particular knowledge system.

Omoluabi distinction: known, observed, classified

This case becomes clearer when three different acts are separated:

  1. Known: local communities recognized an animal and had a name for it before the 2026 paper.
  2. Observed and documented: a living individual reached Senda Verde Animal Refuge after being found near forest, and researchers began comparing its appearance with other tiger cats.
  3. Formally classified: genomic comparison across Leopardus specimens produced evidence strong enough for researchers to propose Leopardus tilcayo as a distinct species in Current Biology.

Those are not interchangeable claims. Omoluabi should not erase the first two merely because the third has the greatest authority inside scientific institutions.

What changed when science recognized it?

The most consequential change may not be the Latin name. It is the unit of measurement.

If tiger cats across a huge portion of South and Central America are treated as one species, their combined geographic range can make the group appear relatively widespread. If genomic evidence separates that population into several species, each lineage has a smaller range and population to assess. That can change conservation priorities, threat estimates and the urgency assigned to habitat loss.

National Geographic quotes researchers making precisely this point: once one broad category becomes multiple species, each one has to be evaluated on its own. Reuters reports that the new taxonomy has been shared with the IUCN for future assessment. Formal recognition therefore has material consequences even when local recognition came first.

The authority stack

The tilcayo moved through several systems of authority before becoming global news:

local memory → local name → individual encounter → sanctuary record → field-biologist doubt → visual comparison → museum specimens → whole-genome analysis → peer-reviewed taxonomy → media amplification → conservation reassessment

Each layer can see something the others cannot. Local memory can preserve continuity across generations. A sanctuary can preserve an individual animal. Museum collections can preserve biological material across decades. Genomics can expose evolutionary separation invisible to the eye. Peer review can test a scientific claim. Conservation institutions can translate classification into protection frameworks.

The problem begins when the later layers are narrated as if they produced all earlier knowledge themselves.

What the genome did — and did not — prove

The genomic evidence addresses a specific question: whether the Bolivian lineage is evolutionarily distinct from other tiger cats. Published reporting says the research compared complete genomes from 38 individuals across the Leopardus genus and supported five distinct tiger-cat species.

That does not mean the genome answers every question about L. tilcayo. Researchers still lack a reliable population estimate, a complete range map and detailed knowledge of reproduction, prey, predators and habitat requirements. Reuters reports evidence from only two examined living individuals plus limited camera-trap records. Preliminary observations suggest nocturnal activity and small rodents in the diet.

Omoluabi status: species distinction is strongly evidenced; population size, full geographic range and much of the animal's ecology remain unresolved.

Museums are part of this story too

The study's use of museum specimens matters. Collections can preserve biological evidence that becomes newly legible when technology changes. A specimen collected under one classification can later become evidence against that classification.

That makes a museum not just a storage site but a delayed evidence system. Omoluabi should ask where each specimen came from, who collected it, how it was labeled, whether its provenance is complete and what communities or ecosystems are represented without having had control over the resulting archive.

The present sources establish the scientific use of museum material, but they do not by themselves answer the broader provenance questions. Those remain lines of investigation rather than conclusions.

The conservation paradox

A species can become scientifically more visible at the same moment its vulnerability becomes more visible.

The Yungas are under pressure from deforestation, agriculture and mining. Before a lineage is formally separated, damage to its habitat may be absorbed statistically into a much larger species range. After separation, the same habitat loss can look far more consequential.

This is why taxonomy is not only a naming exercise. Classification determines what governments, NGOs, databases and international conservation bodies count.

Historical erasure test

A conventional version of this story can unintentionally produce a false timeline:

2026: scientists discover a new cat.

The evidence supports a more complete sequence:

unknown date: local knowledge and the name “tilcayo” already exist → 2016: a sanctuary encounter brings one individual into sustained scientific attention → 2019: morphological mismatch becomes more apparent → subsequent genomic work tests the distinction → 2026: formal species description is published.

The missing date at the beginning is itself important. We currently know that the local name predates the scientific description, but the sources reviewed here do not establish exactly how far back that knowledge extends. Omoluabi should preserve that uncertainty rather than invent a date.

So who discovered Leopardus tilcayo?

There is no need to force a single answer onto different acts of knowing.

Local communities knew an animal called tilcayo. A local encounter brought an individual to a sanctuary. A Bolivian researcher recognized that the accepted classification might be wrong. A multinational scientific team assembled genomic and museum evidence sufficient to formally describe a distinct species. Journalists then translated that taxonomic event into a global “discovery” story.

All of those statements can be true at the same time.

The Omoluabi intervention is to preserve the provenance between them.

What Omoluabi should investigate next

  1. Local testimony: Who uses the word “tilcayo,” in which communities, and what is remembered about the animal beyond its name?
  2. Specimen provenance: Which museum specimens contributed to the taxonomic revision, where were they collected and how were they originally classified?
  3. Map-data: Plot confirmed observations, camera-trap detections, protected areas, mining concessions, agricultural expansion and forest loss without publishing sensitive coordinates that could increase risk to the species.
  4. Conservation status: Track when and how IUCN or national Bolivian authorities assess the newly described lineage.
  5. Database lag: Measure how quickly major biodiversity databases update after a taxonomic revision and whether older records are remapped correctly.
  6. Benefit and authority: When local knowledge helps identify a species, how are local communities credited, consulted and included in subsequent conservation decisions?

Omoluabi finding

Leopardus tilcayo is a strong proof of concept because it demonstrates that evidence can change not only what we know, but what institutions are capable of seeing. Before the genomic revision, an animal could be present, observed and locally named while remaining hidden inside an overly broad scientific category.

The 2026 paper made the lineage formally visible. Omoluabi's job is to keep that visibility from erasing the knowledge that came before it.
