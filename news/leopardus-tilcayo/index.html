Leopardus tilcayo: The Wild Cat Hidden in Bolivia's Cloud Forest

2026-09-17 · Omoluabi News — Research POC

A newly described tiger cat from Bolivia's Yungas shows how genomics, museum collections, field observation and local knowledge can redraw the map of biodiversity.

A cat that science had not named

On September 17, 2026, researchers formally described Leopardus tilcayo, a small spotted wild cat from Bolivia's Yungas cloud forests. The peer-reviewed study, published in Current Biology, reports that what had often been treated as a single tiger-cat complex contains multiple distinct evolutionary lineages. The Bolivian lineage was described as a new species.

The animal itself was not unknown to people living around it. Local communities already used the name tilcayo. The scientific change was not the first human encounter with the cat; it was the accumulation of evidence strong enough to establish that this population represented a distinct evolutionary lineage.

The discovery chain

National Geographic reports that in 2016 Bolivian biologist Paola Nogales-Ascarrunz was contacted about an unusual cat at a wildlife sanctuary. A local man had found it as a kitten near forest and initially believed it was a domestic cat. At Senda Verde Animal Refuge, the animal's markings raised questions about whether it fit existing descriptions of South American tiger cats.

Appearance alone could not settle the question. Tiger cats are a useful example of the cryptic-species problem: animals can be visually similar while carrying deep genetic differences. The research team therefore compared genomic material across the tiger-cat complex, including living and museum specimens. Reporting on the study describes a dataset of 38 Leopardus specimens.

The resulting phylogenomic analysis supported five distinct tiger-cat species and a newly described Peruvian subspecies, Leopardus tigrinus antisuyo. The lineage leading to L. tilcayo is estimated to have diverged from close relatives roughly 1.4 million years ago.

What is known — and what is not

The best-documented individual highlighted by National Geographic is about 18 inches (roughly 46 centimetres) from head to end of body and weighs about 3 pounds (roughly 1.4 kilograms), smaller than an average domestic cat. Published images show a light-brown coat with dark, irregular rosettes and a relatively slender build.

But the knowledge base remains thin. Reuters reports that researchers have examined only two living individuals plus a small number of camera-trap records. Preliminary observations suggest nocturnal activity, and rodent remains found in scat indicate small rodents may be part of the diet. Scientists do not yet have a reliable population estimate, a complete distribution map, or a detailed account of reproduction, prey, predators and habitat requirements.

Why “first new cat species in more than a century” needs context

Several outlets describe L. tilcayo as the first newly identified living cat species in more than a century. The useful distinction is taxonomic: felid classifications have changed many times as known subspecies were split, merged or elevated. In this case, the researchers describe a lineage that had not previously been formally proposed as its own species or subspecies.

The conservation problem begins immediately

Recognition creates a conservation question at the same moment it solves a taxonomic one. The Yungas are humid mountain forests on the eastern slopes of the Andes. Reuters and other reporting identify deforestation, agricultural expansion and mining among pressures on the ecosystem. If L. tilcayo occupies a narrow range, assessing it separately from a continent-wide tiger-cat category could materially change how its risk is understood.

The research team has shared the revised taxonomy with the International Union for Conservation of Nature. Future assessments may therefore evaluate these lineages independently rather than under older, broader categories.

The Omoluabi test: who gets to say a species exists?

The evidence chain is more revealing than the headline “scientists discover a cat.” Local communities already had a name for the animal. A sanctuary held a living individual. A field biologist noticed a mismatch. Museum material preserved evidence collected before current sequencing methods existed. Genomics then made it possible to compare those fragments across geography and reconstruct an evolutionary history.

Evidence chain: local knowledge → field observation → sanctuary record → morphological contradiction → museum specimens → genome comparison → taxonomic revision → conservation reassessment.

No single layer tells the entire story. That is the point of this Omoluabi proof of concept: preserve who knew what, when they knew it, what kind of evidence changed the classification, and what remains unresolved.

What Omoluabi should watch next

  1. Range: Where exactly does L. tilcayo occur within the Bolivian Yungas?
  2. Population: How many individuals remain, and are populations connected?
  3. Ecology: What does the cat eat, when does it hunt, and which habitats does it require?
  4. Genetic diversity: How much variation exists within the newly recognized species?
  5. Threat overlap: Which forms of land-use change intersect its confirmed habitat?
  6. Conservation status: How will the IUCN assess the lineage when evaluated independently?
  7. Local testimony: What additional names, observations and records exist in communities that have known the animal for generations?

The discovery of Leopardus tilcayo is therefore less an endpoint than the opening of a dataset. The cat has acquired a scientific name. The next task is to build enough knowledge to understand the conditions under which it can persist.
